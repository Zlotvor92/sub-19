# Independent blind running-plan review — 5K coach

Reviewer: `5k`. Blinded: true. Initial review covers all 20 complete plans: U01–U10, X and Y, **324 plan-weeks and 2,268 day entries**. No version identity is inferred. Event responses are outside this review.

## Method and locked scoring

I independently examined profile inputs, every complete weekly day structure, session instructions and parameters, strides, rest/strength placement, long runs, presentation warnings, and the supplied weekly summaries. Summaries were aids to reading rather than evidence that a plan is good: all supplied failure arrays are empty, but substantive coaching concerns remain.

The seven ratings are 0–10 and use the locked weights: fit 20, quality 20, progression 15, recovery 15, specificity 10, taper 10, coherence 10. Total is `2*fit + 2*quality + 1.5*progression + 1.5*recovery + specificity + taper + coherence`. Rough anchors: 9–10 unusually complete and appropriate; 7–8 credible with identifiable compromises; 5–6 substantial development gaps; below 5 inadequacy in that dimension. Decimal precision records judgment and should not be mistaken for measurement accuracy.

Fit means matching delivered work to the athlete's current base, continuity, break, quality experience, available days and aspiration. Quality means useful accumulated work, pacing and recovery for the stated purpose, not merely the presence of intervals or tempo labels. Progression includes changes in work duration, running fraction, long exposure and interruptions by recovery weeks. Recovery includes the whole seven-day pattern and Sunday-to-Monday carryover, with long endurance and fast finishes counted as demands. Specificity is assessed for each plan's own race distance; I do not require the marathon plans to resemble a 5K plan. Taper means actual reductions in running and work, retaining modest rhythm without unnecessary fatigue. Coherence includes athlete-facing instructions, the goal/reference distinction, conditional readiness and alignment of labels with actual workouts.

Weekly JSON notes include every day's role and distance, structured work minutes and an authored coaching assessment. Structured work totals exclude strides. For progressive sessions, only the final third is at the faster pace; a 13.2 km progression is not 13.2 km threshold work. Race-week volume includes the race, so I examine pre-race running separately. Exact sums and labels cannot substitute for practical training adequacy.

Critical defects are separate concern entries. Here a critical concern means a fundamental gap preventing endorsement of the deliverable for its scheduled race. It is not a prediction that harm is inevitable. Only U09 X and U09 Y receive a critical concern: their marathon endurance exposure is severely insufficient relative to the scheduled marathon, and warning prose does not supply a usable alternative.

## Public primary coaching references

Retrieved on 2026-10-12. These are illustrative coaching frameworks, not required templates or proof of a unique correct programme. Judgments below about specific delivered sessions are my coaching inferences.

- [Hal Higdon, Novice 5K](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/5k-training/novice-5k/): a low-mileage, three-run-day approach, conversational running, rest and permission to walk during both training and racing. Its longest prescribed workout is about 4.8 km, illustrating why neither an overdistance long run nor 30 km/week is a universal 5K completion requirement.
- [Hal Higdon, Advanced 5K](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/5k-training/advanced-5k/): greater experience permits more developed speed/endurance training; the public schedule combines intervals, tempo, easy long runs, a fitness test and a rest-oriented race week. I use this to assess purpose and development, not to mandate its frequency or pacing.
- [Boston Athletic Association, Boston 5K training](https://www.baa.org/races/boston-5k/info-for-athletes/boston-5k-training/): the public levels differentiate preparation and accumulated work. Level Two uses repeated race-rhythm sets with explicit recoveries and a substantially shorter taper session. That supports examining the connection between repeat duration, accumulated work and recovery rather than rating all intervals equally.
- [Runna, Ultimate 5K training guide](https://support.runna.com/en/articles/6636429-the-ultimate-5k-training-guide): public guidance describes gradual walk/run transition, predominantly easy running, speed sessions and experience-dependent long runs. I do not assume or claim knowledge of Runna's proprietary plan generator. Its popular easy/hard rule of thumb is not a mandatory physiological ratio for this audit.
- [Runna, Interval training guide](https://support.runna.com/en/articles/9027121-what-are-interval-runs-a-guide-to-interval-training-for-runners): public taper guidance emphasizes shorter repetitions and lower total work while preserving rhythm. It informs the assessment of actual taper work rather than labels.
- [Hal Higdon, Intermediate 10K](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/10k-training/intermediate-10k/): differentiates a performance programme by established experience, comfortable regular running and deliberate faster work. This supports matching the work dose to the experienced profiles, without imposing its prerequisites on every 10K runner.
- [Hal Higdon, Novice 1 half marathon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/half-marathon-training/novice-1-half-marathon/): illustrates four-day completion preparation, progressive long running, easy recovery, walking options and a brief taper. It is a counterexample to treating higher fixed weekly-mileage cutoffs as universal completion requirements.
- [Hal Higdon, Novice 1 marathon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/novice-1-marathon/): a public four-run-day programme builds from six miles to twenty, with stepbacks and a three-week taper. Four days are therefore not intrinsically disqualifying. The relevant concern in U09 is the delivered endurance trajectory, not the day count or a fixed 55 km/week line.

## Key coaching examples

**Novice 5K transition, U01.** Both sides start with small run/walk outings and delay formal quality. Their low mileage is defensible for the starting base, and a 4.6 km longest run is near race distance. I do not deduct merely because they peak below the presentation's approximate 30 km/week benchmark. X week 5 explicitly makes continuous running conditional on conversational effort and no pain, allows repetition of the last walk/run step, and postpones strides. Y changes to continuous running and adds six strides Monday without that check. This makes X easier to endorse for a runner with no established continuity. Week 4 on both sides cuts distance but raises the running fraction from 3:1 to 5:1; it is not a deload in every dimension. Both race instructions would benefit from an explicit permitted walk/run completion option when tolerance remains uncertain. This assessment follows the public novice/walk-run principles above, while the precise scoring is my inference.

**Experienced 5K stimulus, U03.** With 50 km/week, 24 consistent weeks and quality experience, a developed workload is credible. X repeats 2 × 1,200 m intervals (9.48 minutes at 3:57/km) and 2 × 1,600 m threshold (about 14 minutes) through much of the cycle. Y provides 3 × 1,200 m and 3 × 1,600 m, accumulating about 35.26 minutes across two separated days. These are not automatically excessive simply because the minutes are greater: easy running dominates and recovery weeks remain. In week 13, X has 3 × 1 km at reference 5K pace with 185 seconds walking; Y has 4 × 1 km with 90 seconds. Y offers a more convincing race-rhythm rehearsal. This is a specialist coaching inference, not a universal minimum dose.

**Recreational 5K aspiration, U02.** Both sides correctly distinguish the 19:59 wish from the 23:45 result and avoid using the wish to dictate hard training. Nevertheless, very brief opening fartleks, repeated two-repetition intervals and long session padding make the quality development modest. X's shorter week-13 race-rhythm recovery is useful; it does not turn 2 km of work into strong evidence that sub-20 is likely. No scheduled checkpoint verifies the sizeable desired improvement. Preserving conservative reference paces is a strength; a test-and-reassess pathway would be better than pretending the initial result predicts all future progress.

**10K accumulation, U04–U05.** U04 introduces quality gently, but 0.4 km threshold in week 4 is only 2.11 minutes inside a 5.9 km session. It is token threshold maintenance at most. Y's late one-minute walking breaks create more connected 10K rhythm than X's four-minute breaks, although both keep accumulated race work small. U05 X, with established experience, reaches 4.8 km of race work in week 14 through 3 × 1,600 m with 75-second recovery. Y has 3.2 km with four-minute recovery. X provides greater specificity without forcing the aspirational 42-minute goal. Long walking recovery is not inherently wrong; here it weakens a small endurance-rhythm dose.

**Return after a break, U06.** Reduced opening distance and delayed formal quality are appropriate on both sides. X immediately prescribes six 20-second PB-derived strides even though the athlete has an eight-week break, one consistent week and a result a year old. Y removes early strides, explicitly lets conversational effort override old pace, and attaches a logged-tolerance/new-test gate to the first quality session. Those are substantive safeguards. Its 75% completion, RPE, pain and 90-day criteria are operational heuristics, not validated universal readiness laws; a printed plan cannot establish that they have been met. Even Y still peaks at a 6.7 km longest run, so the 10K performance remains unconfirmed.

**HM demand and taper, U07–U08.** U07 X builds earlier connected HM blocks and turns Friday easy as Sunday long exposure becomes more demanding. In week 11, Y retains both a threshold day and HM work whose 1.7 km repeats are separated by 326 seconds easy recovery, while the longest run grows to 18.1 km. The long run matters in assessing demand, even when labelled Z2. X also reduces week-15 structured work to 17.89 minutes; Y retains 28.61 minutes. U08 Y better develops threshold and HM rhythm early, and reduces work in its late taper. X keeps the same 2 × 2.5 km HM session into week 12, producing 41.79 work minutes before another 33.81-minute week. Both have useful controlled rehearsals and fast finishes; medium-long running the day after the rehearsal must remain truly easy or be shortened according to fatigue.

**Marathon readiness, U09.** Both sides peak at only 17.7 km, about 105 minutes, despite a twenty-week cycle and a scheduled 42.195 km race. Stepbacks and local recovery are reasonable, and forcing rapid distance increases would not solve the problem. However, the delivered programme offers severely limited long-duration adaptation and rehearsal for a four-hour marathon, while adding a second formal quality day late. Candid warnings deserve credit for coherence but cannot make this adequate race preparation. The critical concern calls for an actionable date/distance/completion reconsideration; it is not based on four run days or a universal 55 km minimum. A base-building programme can be useful while its originally scheduled race remains inappropriate to endorse.

**Experienced marathon, U10.** Both sides offer substantial repeated long and medium-long exposure and fuel practice, so their assessment differs materially from U09. X has somewhat more developed work, but even its recurring marathon rhythm is only 4.1 km continuous, and Y generally has 3.3 km. The 9.2 km race-pace finish in week 15 is the clearest specific long workout, appearing once. The final pre-race week still contains roughly 18 km-plus Sunday endurance and 36.6 structured work minutes; that may be tolerable for some experienced runners but is not an especially decisive freshness-oriented taper. Y week 18 reduces formal work only from 40.1 to 39.07 minutes even as mileage falls substantially. These are coaching compromises rather than proof of harm.

**Activation semantics across distances.** Several plans call near-race-pace 200 m efforts “Repeticije,” zone R, pace source current-fitness. Their actual pace is close to the race reference, not to the earlier faster R prescription. Other plans accurately describe controlled activation and explicitly avoid sprinting. I mark the purpose/source inconsistency as technical and give modest coherence credit for clearer wording; I do not claim the actual near-race efforts become dangerous merely because of their label.

## Limitations

All profiles are synthetic. Age, injury history, actual easy effort, surfaces, elevation, conditions, sleep, work stress, previous weekly distribution, preferred workout formats and strength-training load are absent. The entered recent long run and mileage may not represent reliably repeatable training. No completion or fatigue evidence establishes future adaptation. Conditional text is assessed as text, not proof that the app enforces a gate; no proprietary implementation is assumed.

The 5K specialist perspective gives greatest confidence to U01–U03; longer-race judgments use general endurance coaching and the public longer-race programmes above. Illustrative programmes vary widely and cannot establish universal limits. Small numerical differences should be interpreted within ordinary reviewer uncertainty. Neither a supplied PB projection nor an “endurance supported” flag guarantees the race result or completion. Conversely, low mileage alone does not establish failure for a short-distance completion goal.

This initial record is independent of other reviewers and remains locked after submission. Later event review must be recorded separately.
