# Independent blinded half-marathon specialist review

Reviewer: `hm`. Initial review, completed before any separate event review. Identifiers X/Y are treated only as blinded plan sides; no version identity was inferred. All 20 complete delivered plans, 324 weeks and 2,268 day entries were inspected. Events were not evaluated. This report and `hm-scores.json` are independently authored.

## Method and locked rubric

The seven 0–10 criteria use the required weights: fit 20, quality 20, progression 15, recovery 15, specificity 10, taper 10, coherence 10. Total = 2×fit + 2×quality + 1.5×progression + 1.5×recovery + specificity + taper + coherence. Approximate anchors: 9–10 excellent fit with little material revision; 7–8 credible outline with identifiable coaching limitations; 5–6 meaningful revision required; 0–4 major target/preparation mismatch. Ratings express independent coaching judgment, not a validated prediction of injury or race performance. A one-point difference on a criterion can be meaningful; small overall differences should not be overinterpreted.

I reviewed the athlete inputs, every week's day ordering, run/walk instructions, easy and hard doses, warm-ups/cool-downs, recoveries, strides, medium-long runs, long-run finishes, non-running days, optional strength, cutbacks, race practice, taper, warning text and goal/reference distinction. Repeated instructions were inspected once in a dictionary and mapped to every day that uses them; complete weekly day schedules were retained in the inspection. The supplied summary was checked after the day review. Session work duration was independently calculated from repetitions × distance × pace, or repetitions × seconds. For progressive runs only the final third is faster work. Long-run fast finishes count as work; strides and the race itself do not count in the weekly work evidence. Recoveries do not count as work. These quantities describe dose, not a universal target intensity distribution.

All weekly totals agree with the sum of prescribed day distances within 0.1 km rounding; every supplied failure list is empty. That is useful arithmetic evidence but does not establish coaching adequacy. The score file has one independently authored note for every week and plan, specific concerns with week/day evidence, and separate critical classifications.

Fit considers declared continuity, break length, quality experience, current mileage, repeatable long run, days available and goal. Recovery considers the entire cross-week sequence, not only adjacent formal quality labels. A long run at easy pace can still be demanding, especially when prolonged or new. I did not impose a universal two-hard-day ceiling, a mandatory 80/20 split, a 10% growth limit, or a 30% long-run share. A stepback followed by resumption of a prior tolerated workload is not judged solely on percentage increase from the stepback. An athlete can use four days successfully for a marathon; the adequacy of delivered long runs is a different question.

## Public primary sources retrieved

These sources are coaching comparators and evidence frameworks, not templates that all plans must reproduce. Only public guidance was used; nothing here assumes Runna's proprietary implementation.

1. [Boston Athletic Association, Boston Half Training](https://www.baa.org/races/boston-half/info-for-athletes/boston-half-training/), retrieved during review. The B.A.A. distinguishes experience levels, uses accumulated half-marathon rhythm and controlled simulations, and advises easy warm-up/cool-down around higher-intensity work. I used these as support for purposeful event-specific practice, rather than copying its mileage or sessions.
2. [Hal Higdon, Novice 1 Half Marathon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/half-marathon-training/novice-1-half-marathon/), retrieved during review. This public plan presumes a basic running base, emphasizes conversational effort, gradually longer endurance runs, and recovery. It illustrates that first-HM adequacy depends on starting capacity and endurance development, not just the existence of speed sessions.
3. [Hal Higdon, Novice 1 Marathon](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/novice-1-marathon/), retrieved during review. Its four-run format, substantial long-run development, stepbacks and taper demonstrate that four available days do not themselves explain a short-long-run marathon plan. Its approximately 32 km longest run is a comparator, not a universally required distance.
4. [Runna, Top Tips for Tapering Before a Race](https://support.runna.com/en/articles/6576215-top-tips-for-tapering-before-a-race), retrieved during review. Public guidance emphasizes reducing training load while retaining small sharpening work, prioritizing freshness, and reducing strength load. I judged taper by actual duration and work reduction, not the taper flag or a proprietary Runna schedule.
5. [Runna, How to Fuel Your Running Training Week](https://support.runna.com/en/articles/6169270-how-to-fuel-your-running-training-week), retrieved during review. This supports treating fueling as part of training and recovery, with demand varying by duration and intensity. It supports credit for rehearsal and long-run fueling reminders; I did not treat every numeric fueling instruction as personalized nutrition clearance.
6. [Bosquet et al., 2007, Effects of tapering on performance: a meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/), DOI [10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0), retrieved during review. The authors' synthesis supports volume reduction with retained intensity/frequency in competitive athletes. Its roughly two-week and 41–60% findings are a framework with population limits, not rigid requirements for every beginner or race distance.

For example, the very small beginner U01 doses are sensible; the similarly small experienced U03-X and U05-Y doses are less convincing as performance development. U07-X removing a second formal workout as long runs increase is a positive recovery decision even though U07-Y often retains more quality.

## Half-marathon findings

**U07, first HM.** Both sides grow a repeatable 10 km long run to 19.5 km and introduce fueling practice. That is credible endurance preparation when tolerated, and does not require the runner to cover race distance in training. X separates threshold from HM rhythm early: week 5 uses 2×1.4 km threshold and 2×1 km HM rhythm with 90-second jog recovery. Weeks 9–11 then prioritize longer endurance outings with only one formal quality session. This is better aligned with a first HM than perpetuating two generic speed/threshold sessions solely because the requested quality count is two.

Y's first HM-rhythm blocks appear in week 10, with 5:26 jogging recovery between 1.7 km repetitions. Those recoveries are almost as long as half a repetition and weaken continuity for an athlete preparing to sustain approximately 109 minutes of running. The criticism is specific to the intended rhythm stimulus; generous recovery can be useful for other purposes. Both sides' 7 km rehearsal is clearly controlled and explicitly does not prove new fitness. X's week-15 work is about 17.9 minutes versus Y's 28.6 minutes, while both retain a 12.6 km long run. The fuller Y dose deserves less taper credit. Neither side earns confirmation of the desired 1:45 finish merely from the 5K reference.

**U08, experienced HM.** Both supply repeated 22 km long runs, useful medium-long endurance, a 10 km controlled rehearsal and 5.5 km HM fast finishes. Y's early threshold sequence is substantially more useful: week 1 provides 4 km broken threshold plus 3 km continuous threshold, progressing to longer blocks and HM rhythm with 60-second jog recoveries. X starts with only 3.4 km combined threshold and devotes 6.5 km of each quality outing to warm-up/cool-down. This is mostly aerobic maintenance, not persuasive experienced-HM threshold development.

The fast-finish weeks correctly make Friday easy, so the long-run finish is recognized as a major stimulus rather than silently creating a third formal workout. Y's taper reduces HM and threshold doses from week 12 into week 13. X retains full 5 km HM blocks and returns to a second weekday threshold session during its first taper week. That density weakens freshness despite the taper label. Both schedule a 13.6 km medium-long immediately after the 10 km controlled rehearsal. Its intensity is easy, so the accompanying “next 2–3 days easy” instruction is technically followed, but approximately 73 minutes of running may still be too much recovery work for some athletes. That is a coaching concern, not a claimed universal violation.

## Other key examples and critical defects

U01-X gives an actionable choice: continuous running is a trial only after comfortable pain-free run/walk, otherwise repeat the prior step. U01-Y's same distances become continuous unconditionally and include six strides on the first continuous Monday. That matters more than whether total kilometers reconcile. Both begin conservatively and distinguish a 30-minute wish from the 35-minute reference.

U06 has an eight-week break, only one consistent week, and a year-old 5K. X correctly reduces mileage and warns about the stale PB, but immediately supplies strides at an old-result-derived pace and later introduces quality by calendar. Y removes the first three weeks' strides, prioritizes conversational effort, makes historical pace an orientation, and attaches tolerance/test conditions to the first quality introduction. The operational numeric conditions are not universal physiological clearance. Y's race-week shakeouts should keep the same effort-first instruction. Both top out at only 6.7 km longest run; the low volume is not an absolute prohibition against finishing a 10K, but makes confident time-target endorsement inappropriate.

Experienced U03-Y and U05-X have more credible work duration and late race-rhythm accumulation. The corresponding other sides commonly prescribe only two long interval repetitions, then heavily interrupt small accumulated race-pace doses with lengthy walking recoveries. Labels cannot replace purpose: race-week “R” sessions here are near race pace, substantially slower than the plans' earlier repetition pace. A short near-race-rhythm activation is sensible, but the label should match that intention. This is a technical/coherence issue with limited independent physiological consequence, not a critical defect.

**Critical classification: U09-X and U09-Y, delivered marathon-preparation mismatch.** A 20-week preparation never exceeds 17.7 km or approximately 105 minutes of long running, yet culminates in a 42.195 km race with a roughly four-hour pace reference. There are repeated small threshold sessions and very short marathon blocks, but no substantial longer event-duration exposure. I would not endorse either as a complete marathon preparation plan. The warnings are candid and receive coherence credit; they do not supply an alternative race/date or repair the delivered preparation. The critical classification refers to the magnitude of the preparation/task mismatch, not a prediction that this individual will be injured or fail. It is independent of the overall numerical score. A four-day schedule can support marathon preparation; a fixed 55 km peak threshold is not the reason for the classification.

U10 is different: the athlete reports a 60 km base, 24 km recent long run, established quality and marathon experience. Repeated 28–29.3 km long runs and substantial medium-long runs are credible endurance support. No universal 32 km or three-hour rule is imposed. However, numerous approximately three-hour long runs with medium-long and quality work need explicit response-based flexibility. Much weekday marathon work is only 3.2–4.1 km, with the 9.2 km fast finish the strongest specific exposure. The 18.2–18.7 km long run seven days before the marathon lasts roughly 112–115 minutes; a shorter option would improve freshness. The recent marathon PB is 22 days before plan start, so recovery cannot be inferred from the result alone, though the stated current base is respected.

## Limitations and interpretation

These are synthetic profiles, not observed athletes. Age, injury history, actual weekly distribution, long-run tolerance, race-course conditions, available session duration, sleep, strength history, fueling tolerance and post-run recovery are missing. “Experienced” does not identify precisely what work was recently tolerated. No completed training logs were available for the initial review. The plan cannot prove its own completion, and an empty log is neither proof of success nor failure.

The source plans show multiple valid training methods. They do not establish that a specific threshold dose, recovery ratio, long-run share, eight-week continuity cutoff, or race-equivalence formula is a universal biological law. Race equivalence from a 5K can be useful for orientation while remaining insufficient to establish HM endurance. Delivered planned volume is a prescription, not completed preparation. The stronger HM plans still require effort-based adjustment and cannot guarantee race-time goals.

The saved scores are locked initial judgments. No separate event outcomes, repository, manifest, git history, prior QA, other-reviewer conclusions, or hidden plan identities informed them.
