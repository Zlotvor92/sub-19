# Independent blind marathon-specialist review

## Method

I independently inspected the complete seven-day structure of every week of all twenty X/Y plans U01–U10, including day descriptions, structured sessions, strides, run/walk stages, warm-up/cooldown, repetition length, work duration, recovery mode and duration, long and medium-long runs, race week, presentation warnings, goal/reference distinction and preparation status. I used only the supplied blind directory and retrieved public primary coaching sources. No repository, git, manifest, events, previous QA or other reviewer material informed this review. The initial raw U01 read also exposed event metadata embedded in the athlete object; no event plan or event response was evaluated. Subsequent extraction used only athlete input and initial plans.

Ratings follow the locked seven-criterion rubric: fit 20, quality 20, progression 15, recovery 15, specificity 10, taper 10, coherence 10. Total = 2*fit + 2*quality + 1.5*progression + 1.5*recovery + specificity + taper + coherence. Scores are coaching judgments, not validated outcome probabilities; differences of a point should not be overinterpreted. I assessed each complete plan on its own terms and then recorded side-specific distinctions without assigning version identity. In otherwise identical weeks the same judgment is retained. Every weekly note states the inspected day pattern and a coaching assessment.

A quality label is not enough: work time, recovery and experience determine purpose. For progressive runs only the final third counts as faster work. A fast-finish long run is a demanding session, not an easy run solely because its tag is LR. Weekly sums were checked against days; race distance is excluded when assessing pre-race taper volume. Race-inclusive volume can exceed prior training without implying a taper defect. The observed approximately 0.005 km race-week rounding difference is immaterial and was not penalized.

Critical defects remain separate from numerical scores. Here critical means an event-preparation defect that prevents marathon readiness sign-off, not a diagnosis or a prediction that injury will occur. Warnings receive credit for honesty but cannot supply missing physiological preparation. I do not recommend filling an endurance gap with a sudden late jump.

## Retrieved primary sources and use

- B.A.A. Boston Marathon training: https://www.baa.org/races/boston-marathon/info-for-athletes/boston-marathon-training/ — public level-one example uses four running days with much longer endurance preparation than the constrained U09 block. Its marathon-specific examples include sustained MP work and two-week taper. Used as an example of plausible event-specific design, not a mandatory mileage floor.
- Hal Higdon Novice 1 marathon: https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/novice-1-marathon/ — eighteen-week, four-run structure, long-run development and stepbacks, with the peak long run before a three-week taper. Supports the importance of accumulating long-run durability; does not establish that every athlete must run exactly 20 miles.
- Hal Higdon Intermediate 1 marathon: https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/intermediate-1-marathon/ — five-day experienced-runner example, longer easy endurance work and pace practice. Supports separating easy long-run effort from race rhythm.
- B.A.A. Boston 5K training: https://www.baa.org/races/boston-5k/info-for-athletes/boston-5k-training/ — official level-two example provides repeated controlled race-related work with warm-up/cooldown and taper reduction. Helps contextualize experienced 5 km plans with repeated sub-ten-minute interval doses; it is not a universal minimum.
- Hal Higdon Intermediate 2 half marathon: https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/half-marathon-training/intermediate-2-half-marathon/ — official experienced-runner schedule includes easy conversational running, speed/tempo, sustained race-pace practice and progressive long runs. Illustrates several valid ways to prepare a half marathon; its taper differs from the provided plans, so I did not require exact matching.
- Bosquet et al., 2007, Effects of tapering on performance: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/ — retrieved original meta-analysis abstract gives a framework of reduced volume with retained intensity/frequency. Applies as a framework, not an exact rule for every novice or distance; I examined actual hard-work reduction and long-run duration.
- Runna public nutrition guidance: https://support.runna.com/en/articles/11646849-how-to-train-your-gut-ahead-of-race-day — supports progressive carbohydrate tolerance practice with familiar products and hydration. Credits the plans for rehearsing fuel; no proprietary Runna plan logic was assumed.

## Key examples

**U09 X and Y — critical preparation defect.** Both twenty-week schedules peak at 17.7 km, roughly 105 minutes at prescribed easy-long pace, before a 42.195 km race at about four hours. The issue is the combination of a 10 km reported repeatable long run, limited delivered endurance progression, repeated retreats and very short MP portions; it is not simply failing an arbitrary 20-mile rule or choosing four days. Official B.A.A. and Higdon examples demonstrate that four days can support marathon preparation. Both sides state readiness is unconfirmed and advise revisiting base, days or date. Those warnings are valuable, but the complete scheduled marathon remains beyond demonstrated delivered preparation. These plans are defensible as constrained base work, not as coach-approved marathon readiness.

**U10 X and Y — different from U09.** A stated 24 km repeatable long run, 60 km/week base, recent marathon result and 32 weeks continuity are followed by repeated approximately three-hour long runs and medium-long support. That provides plausible durability without demanding a universally mandatory 32 km long run. The main weaknesses are repeated short MP segments, a long plateau of nearly identical templates and final pre-race weeks retaining around 52–53 km with an approximately 112–115-minute long run and two quality sessions. The 9.2 km fast-finish long-run segment is a valuable exception. X has more sustained quality through the main build; both sides need response-guided recovery and more deliberate MP development. Five three-hour runs across eight weeks are a substantial commitment, not inherently excessive for every experienced runner.

**U07 and U08 — sustained specificity matters.** U07 X builds threshold and HM work with brief easy jog recoveries and reduces late hard-day frequency as long runs grow. Y repeatedly uses small generic interval doses until later and supplies 326-second recoveries in HM repeats. U08 Y gives the experienced runner earlier sustained threshold and HM work and trims hard work more clearly in taper. X begins with only 8.8 and 6.1 minutes threshold despite an established 55 km base, then retains substantial hard work during taper. Both sides of U08 have good long-run endurance and controlled rehearsal intent. The medium-long day immediately after rehearsal and long run near race still deserve a fatigue-sensitive alternative.

**U03 and U05 — conservatism can underserve experience.** U03 X repeatedly gives only about 9.5 minutes of interval work to an experienced 50 km athlete; Y provides more purposeful development and better race-pace continuity. U05 X supplies meaningful race work with short recovery, while Y uses four-minute walking recoveries and smaller work doses despite large easy volume. Neither judgment assumes that more intensity is automatically better or that any fixed work-minute threshold applies to all athletes.

**U01 and U06 — instructions change the coaching prescription.** U01 X makes continuous running conditional and offers the previous run/walk step as fallback, whereas Y changes to continuous running and strides without that local gate. U06 Y removes initial strides, explicitly makes easy effort conversational, treats old pace as a guide and attaches readiness conditions to quality; X mainly offers a general warning while prescribing year-old PB pace on the calendar. Y's 75%, pain, result-age and logged-effort conditions are operational rules, not universal physiological truths. Both U06 plans remain a return block with limited 10 km durability, rather than proof of readiness.

Race-week near-reference 200 m work can be useful activation. Sides that explicitly name activation and its controlled intent are clearer than those labelling the same near-race rhythm R/current-fitness; the latter is mostly a coherence/provenance issue, not inherently a dangerous workout.

## Limitations

The athlete inputs are synthetic and omit age, injury/medical history, baseline daily distribution, fatigue, sleep, terrain, strength-training tolerance, weather, nutrition and session-response data. No outcomes establish actual adaptation. The recent-results references for longer distances can be model projections from 5 km, not demonstrated endurance at those distances. A preparation label such as supported is a conditional planning interpretation, not a promise of finishing or achieving a goal. Unchanged VDOT and pace alone are not failures: future progress needs confirmation rather than automatic speed escalation.

Internal mileage, continuity and pain thresholds were not accepted as universal readiness tests. Low weekly volume does not categorically bar a 5 km finish, nor does one long run prove marathon readiness. Published plans vary widely; source comparisons informed my reasoning rather than mechanically scoring distance conformity. Runna sources were limited to public coaching advice. No event adaptation was reviewed in these initial files. Detailed strengths, concerns, severities, evidence and all 324 weekly notes are preserved in the score JSON.
